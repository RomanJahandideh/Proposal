\section*{Summary}

This research asks what happens to people's creative contributions when a
generative AI system turns them into part of a shared artwork. In small-group
improvisation workshops, participants will type short phrases that a
language model turns into light, form and sound in a shared projected scene.
The study follows how the group recognises, accepts, changes or refuses these
transformations, and who comes to be credited and held responsible for the
result. It contributes an explanation of when a system's record of who
contributed what matches, and when it fails to match, how collaborators
themselves understand authorship.

\section{Introduction}

In collaborative creative tools, a generative model does more than produce
content: it \emph{reinterprets} what people contribute. Someone types a
phrase, and a moment later it has become a sound or a moving form in a shared
scene. The group then has to decide what that transformation means, and
whether it still belongs to the person who offered it. When AI translates
contributions into shared audiovisual material, its outputs may become more
visible or influential than the contributions that prompted them, a
possibility to investigate rather than an established effect. This raises a
cultural question about whose interpretations gain authority, whose
contributions remain recognisable, and how disagreement is negotiated in
collective creative practice. Creative platforms increasingly record who
supplied what; this project asks whether such records settle questions of
credit.

Improvisation is a good setting for the question. Improvisers already have
conventions for listening to, building on and refusing one another's offers,
and improvisation has been argued to be a research method grounded in risk,
active listening and collaborative response \citep{caines2020fragile}. A
system that transforms and labels offers enters those conventions, and may
quietly change them. The project uses the program's research-creation
option: I will build a shared audiovisual environment for improvisation and
study the works made with it.

\section{Objectives}

\subsection{Research problem and questions}

The problem is the relationship between technical attribution, meaning the
system's record of who supplied what, and social recognition, meaning how
collaborators acknowledge, credit and hold one another responsible. Three
working definitions organise the study. \textbf{Recognition}: a contribution
is noticed or acknowledged as meaningful. \textbf{Creative authority}: a
person's interpretation or decision carries influence over what happens next.
\textbf{Authorship}: participants associate ownership, credit or
responsibility with the work, as felt ownership, public attribution and
creative responsibility. Legal ownership and copyright are outside the scope
of this study. These can come apart: a contributor may be credited while the
group reshapes the material regardless of their view (credit without
influence), and a facilitator, interpreter or designer may shape the work
uncredited (influence without credit). The definitions are sensitising
concepts, not fixed categories. Participation includes listening,
facilitation, silence and refusal as well as submitting material.

\textbf{Central question.} In collaborative digital improvisation, how do
participants, facilitators and a generative-AI interface shape whether a
person's contribution is recognised, carries creative authority, and is
credited in the shared work?

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Recognition.} How do participants notice, interpret and respond
        to differences between what they offered and how it was transformed?
  \item \emph{Authority.} Through which practices, including acceptance,
        revision, refusal, facilitation and silence, do contributors and
        groups gain or lose influence over transformed material, and how do
        interface permissions shape those practices?
  \item \emph{Authorship.} When and why do the system's technical attribution
        and participants' accounts of ownership, credit and responsibility
        diverge?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Theoretical perspective}

The primary framework is Lucy Suchman's study of human--machine
reconfiguration \citep{suchman2007}, which examines how agencies are figured
at the interface and how humans and machines come to be treated as similar or
different in practice. On this view, whether a transformed element belongs to
its contributor, to the group or to the system is worked out in interaction.
Suppose the system records that an amber pulse came from one participant's
phrase. The pulse also depends on the model's reading, on the asset library
I built and on the approval rule in force. When the contributor refuses a
request to brighten it, they enact an authority the interface granted, and
the group can accept or contest it. The question shifts from ``who really
made the pulse?'' to how agency is distributed and attributed in that
moment. Three questions guide the analysis: how participants attribute agency
and authorship in talk and action; how interface rules, model defaults,
facilitation and my design decisions make some actions easy and others
difficult; and who is held responsible when a transformation misrepresents
someone. Improvisation studies adds a limited lens on how offers are taken
up, ignored or refused \citep{caines2020fragile}. These concepts direct
attention without fixing the findings.

\subsection{Expected contribution and originality}

The study investigates whether technical attribution and social recognition
can diverge in collective creative work. A provenance log may record who
supplied a phrase while collaborators treat the transformed material as
another person's, the group's, or nobody's. The intended contribution is an
explanation of how contributors gain or lose authority over transformed
material, and of how interface permissions, facilitation and group
conventions shape that process. It does not offer a general claim that more
individual control produces fairer collaboration. The evidence may help
explain credit without influence, influence without credit, how accepted
material becomes collective, and when provenance fails to resolve authorship
disputes; these are possibilities, not predicted findings. If AI proves unnecessary
in some arrangements, the contribution would lie in explaining when and why.

Originality lies in the combination examined, not in any single element.
Prior work has studied group negotiation around a shared AI
\citep{xu2026}, improvisation as a basis for AI partners and performers
\citep{trajkova2024,mathewson2017}, and observers' attribution of credit for
AI art \citep{epstein2020}. This project follows identifiable contributions
through AI-mediated transformation in \emph{repeated} collective
improvisation, with a human-mediated contrast and attention to facilitation,
and compares technical attribution with socially negotiated recognition. The
resulting concepts may help interpret other platforms that rely on
attribution records, without establishing how those platforms behave.

\section{Methods and sources}

\subsection{Research methods}

The project follows the program's research-creation option, a creative work
with a 150--200-page written dissertation \citep{comcult_rc}. It is closest
to what \citet{chapman2012} call ``research-from-creation'': making the work
generates material for understanding how participants engage with it.

\textbf{The creative work.} The creative component will comprise the shared
audiovisual environment and the improvised works produced through it.
Developing and revising its expressive possibilities, interaction rules and
workshop scores is part of the inquiry into how creative authority is
distributed. Participants build and perform short projected improvisations of
light, form and sound, then discuss them; each rule the environment embodies
is a proposition about collective authorship that they can test, resist or
rewrite. In an illustrative session, four improvisers receive a score: build
the weather of a city none of them has visited. One types ``the bus that
never comes.'' The system proposes a slow amber pulse at the edge of the
space with a low drone, with the generated interpretation ``waiting as a
distant repeating light.'' The contributor accepts it. Later another
participant wants the pulse brighter; the contributor declines, and the
facilitator asks the group how to proceed.

\textbf{Ownership and reuse.} Recording origins and giving contributors
approval rights could introduce individual ownership where improvisers treat
accepted offers as shared. The scoping interviews and co-design described
below address this directly, and one option to test is a way to
``release'' accepted material to the group.

\textbf{The system.} A participant submits a short text phrase. An existing
language model returns one proposal drawn from a curated library of forms,
light, colour, particle ``weather'', sound and text, with a one-line
generated interpretation that is model output, not an explanation of why the
model produced the transformation. Contributors or groups can accept, edit,
reject, request another proposal or undo, and a manual path to the same
library is always open. Generative AI is included because machine-mediated
reinterpretation is the object of study. The project does not presume that
AI improves improvisation. The model does not decide whether anything enters
the work, who is credited or what is removed, and makes no inferences about
participants' emotions or behaviour. Unity applies only permitted, bounded
actions; model output is checked against them and never run as code. No
model is trained or fine-tuned.

\needspace{4\baselineskip}
\textbf{Phases.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Scoping interviews} with improvisers and facilitators about how
        offers, credit, reuse and refusal already work, including whether
        accepted material becomes the group's and who decides that.
  \item \emph{Co-design} with a few practitioners to set the input form, the
        limits of transformation, the starting approval and attribution
        rules, and accessibility provisions. Co-design will examine whether
        contributor approval and visible attribution support practitioners'
        conventions or introduce individualised ownership assumptions they
        do not share. These practitioners will not join the workshop groups.
  \item \emph{Workshops.} Provisionally, three groups of three to five adult
        practitioners each attend four workshops of approximately 90--120
        minutes. The first uses only the manual path, and the group writes
        down its own decision procedure. Each later workshop uses one
        arrangement, in a different order for each group:
        \emph{contributor-led} (AI proposes, contributor approves);
        \emph{group-led} (AI proposes, the group applies its procedure);
        \emph{human-mediated} (a participant in a rotating interpreter role
        proposes with the same assets, contributor approves).
  \item \emph{Reflection interviews} with each participant, reviewing video
        of their own sessions.
\end{enumerate}

Each group meets each arrangement once. What repeats is the group's evolving
collaboration, not trials within an arrangement. The arrangements are
situated contrasts whose effects cannot be separated from order, prior
relationships and emerging norms. I treat these as contextual evidence, and
the study will not isolate a causal effect of AI. During a session,
participants may negotiate how rules apply, for instance by releasing
material to the group or overruling an approval. Substantial system changes
are made only between sessions, and each rule version, its reason and its
consequences are recorded. A changed arrangement is not treated as
equivalent to its earlier version; the change is analysed as evidence of
negotiated authority.

\textbf{Analysis.} I will construct multimodal accounts of selected episodes,
linking video of participants' words and actions with screen recordings and
the system's log. An episode runs from a contribution, through its
transformation and the group's response, to the next creative action.
Selection includes routine acceptance and non-response as well as explicit
disagreement. Selection decisions are documented, and episodes are compared
across groups, arrangements and stages of collaboration, including cases
that challenge emerging interpretations. The episode analysis adapts the
video microethnography \citet{mcarthur2015} used to study interface
interactions. Reflection interviews are analysed through reflexive thematic
analysis \citep{braun2019}, whose themes contextualise episodes without
settling their meaning. System records show what happened in the software,
not a ``true'' account of intention. Discrepant cases revise an emerging
explanation or limit its scope. I will keep an analytic trail rather
than seek inter-rater agreement, and a design journal records how my choices
as designer and facilitator shape what participants can do.

\textbf{Ethics.} Consent will be specific, ongoing and revisable
\citep{im2021,strengers2021}, given separately for recording, AI processing,
use of recordings in the examination, and public dissemination; public
sharing is optional. Provenance
will support identifying and, where feasible, removing attributable
contributions. Participants will agree on withdrawal procedures and
deadlines beforehand, including the limits of removing contributions from
jointly developed or publicly disseminated works. Participants are paid and
credit is agreed with them; data are minimised, pseudonymised and kept for
stated periods. The program's ethics form will be submitted with this
proposal. Full
participation without AI is a design commitment the workshops will test,
through reduced motion, resizable text, alternatives to sound-only
information, facilitator-operated controls and the manual path.
Participation alone does not make a project equitable \citep{birhane2022}.

\subsection{Sources}

The primary sources are generated by the study. They comprise interview
recordings and transcripts from scoping and reflection interviews; video and
screen recordings of workshops; the system's log of contributions,
proposals and decisions; written group procedures and rule versions; the
design journal; and the improvised works themselves. The creative component
will be documented for examination through the executable environment,
workshop scores, selected consented recordings, and an annotated account of
design revisions. Secondary sources are the scholarship discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:lit}.

\subsection{Anticipated problems of access}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Participants and setting.} A possible primary context is a
        connection to Dr.\ Rebecca Caines's SSHRC Insight project,
        \emph{Interfacing, Playtesting, Resetting: Reframing Technology Use
        Through Improvised Creative Technologies} \citep{yfile2026sshrc}.
        Only its title and funding are public, and any relationship would be
        determined with her. Otherwise I will recruit through open calls to
        Toronto improvisation, theatre and sound communities and to AMPD
        students, excluding anyone I teach. As in \citet{chalk2022},
        recruitment will be iterative, checking whose practices are missing
        after the first group and reporting remaining gaps.
  \item \emph{Communities.} I will not work with disability-focused or
        Indigenous communities without established partnerships, community
        governance and relevant expertise.
  \item \emph{AI services.} I will first test a local open-weight model to
        avoid third-party processing. If it is inadequate, I will use only an
        institutionally approved hosted service that receives only the
        phrase, subject to ethics review.
  \item \emph{Retention.} If a group cannot complete all four workshops, its
        completed sessions remain usable as a smaller case, and recruitment
        of a replacement group will be considered.
\end{itemize}

\section{Relationship to existing literature and practice}\label{sec:lit}

\textbf{Stream.} The project contributes to the Technology in Practice
stream, with Media \& Culture as a minor, at the meeting point of
human--machine communication, participatory cultural production and
research-creation.

\textbf{Dominant perspectives.} Human--AI co-creation research in HCI
typically models how people and systems share control. Recent work extends
this to groups: \citet{xu2026} observed six student groups of three working
around one shared ChatGPT interface in a single session and found AI output
becoming a negotiable part of group decisions. They note that their analysis
gave less attention to trust and accountability. Work on AI and
improvisation positions AI as a partner or performer.
\citet{trajkova2024} studied dancers' partnered improvisation to design an
AI partner whose recommendations had not yet been tested.
\citet{mathewson2017} report on live performance with chatbot improvisors.
Studies of credit, such as \citet{epstein2020}, show through vignette
experiments that framing AI as an agent shifts the responsibility observers
assign to people. Contestability has mainly been developed for high-stakes
algorithmic decisions \citep{lyons2021}. Communication and cultural
scholarship explains why recognition matters. \citet[p.~3]{jenkins2006}
define a participatory culture as one in which ``members believe their
contributions matter, and feel some degree of social connection with one
another.'' \citet{chalk2022} shows improvised collective play as cultural
production sustained by non-economic values such as participation,
enjoyment and community. \citet{neville2026} shows how technologies are
rolled out into practices that outsiders misrecognise as non-technical.

\textbf{Where this work fits and what it challenges.} The project takes from
cultural scholarship the stakes of recognition. It questions whether a
system's record of contribution can stand in for recognition. Against
HCI work focused on control and decision-making, it asks how authorship and
responsibility are negotiated over time, by the people making the work. It
also treats the model and the asset library as sources of interpretation,
not neutral containers. Documented challenges include stereotyped images
from text-to-image systems \citep{bianchi2023}, value profiles in five GPT
models resembling English-speaking and Protestant European survey responses
\citep{tao2024}, and a preference for Western-associated entities among
multilingual and Arabic models prompted in Arabic \citep{naous2024}. A 2022
benchmark found models of that time below human accuracy on paired
figurative phrases \citep{liu2022}. These results concern specific models
and tasks, and I will not benchmark models. Instead, I will record how
metaphor, idiom and culturally specific phrases are read when participants
use them, and how my asset choices privilege particular associations. In
professional practice, the project builds on research on improvisation as
methodology and socially responsive creative technologies
\citep{caines2020fragile,caines2023consultation}. It also builds on recent
research-creation on generative AI in teaching, which attends to ethics,
access and improvisatory skills \citep{caines2026steaming}. 

\textbf{Relationship to my master's research.} My master's research at Simon
Fraser University asks how interfaces make a person's state perceptible to
others, through label-free heart-rate cues for remote teammates in a
cooperative Unity game and a Unity environment that responds to the
player's heart rate. Both manuscripts are under revision following peer
review \citep{jahandideh_bioterrarium_ms,jahandideh_teammate_ms}. In the
teammate study, participants tied their willingness to use the cue to
control over it, asking for opt-in sharing, an immediate off switch and
separate permissions \citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms}. As the designer, I also
noticed that the classification behind the display still sorted people into
evaluative categories they never saw. Both lead to this dissertation's
question, who controls how a shared system interprets people, now asked of
deliberate creative contributions rather than bodily signals.

\section{Proposed chapter outline}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
  \item Introduction: AI mediation and whose contribution counts.
  \item Literature and framework: co-creation, credit, contestation and
        human--machine reconfiguration.
  \item Methodology: research-creation, workshops and reflexivity.
  \item The environment as a creative work and the ownership its design
        assumes.
  \item Recognition and authority: how contributions are noticed,
        transformed, taken up or refused.
  \item Authorship: attribution, recognition and responsibility, and where
        they diverge.
  \item Conclusion.
\end{enumerate}
