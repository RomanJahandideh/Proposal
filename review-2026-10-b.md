3. SHARPEN THE INTENDED CONTRIBUTION — SECTION 11

“An account of how creative authority is negotiated” is too general by itself.

Develop the distinction between technical attribution and social recognition. A provenance log may identify who supplied a phrase while collaborators treat the transformed material as belonging to another person, the group, or nobody individually.

Integrate this suggested wording, adapting it naturally:

“The project investigates whether technical attribution and social recognition can diverge in collective creative work. It will examine how contributors gain or lose authority over transformed material, and how interface permissions, facilitation, and group conventions shape that process. Its intended contribution is an explanation of these relationships, rather than a general claim that more individual control produces fairer collaboration.”

Present this as a proposition to investigate, not a predetermined finding. Connect it to the research questions, theory, and evidence.

4. EXAMINE WHETHER THE SYSTEM MANUFACTURES OWNERSHIP — SECTIONS 6–7

The interface identifies contribution origins and gives contributors approval rights. These choices could encourage individual ownership even where improvisers understand material as collectively available.

Address:
- Do practitioners ordinarily credit individual offers?
- Does acceptance make material available to the group?
- Can others build on accepted material without renewed permission?
- Who decides when an element has become collective?
- Does visible attribution alter existing creative conventions?

Integrate:

“Scoping interviews will investigate how practitioners already understand ownership and reuse of improvised offers. Co-design will examine whether contributor approval and visible attribution support their practices or introduce individualised ownership assumptions that participants do not share.”

Keep the design open to revision after scoping. Do not present individual approval rights as automatically fair or culturally appropriate.

5. DISTINGUISH RECOGNITION, AUTHORITY, AND AUTHORSHIP — SECTIONS 3–4

Use working definitions:

Recognition:
A contribution is noticed or acknowledged as meaningful.

Creative authority:
A person’s interpretation or decision carries influence in what happens next.

Authorship:
Participants associate ownership, credit, or responsibility with the work.

Explain how someone may be credited without having influence, or influence the work without being credited.

Use these as sensitising concepts rather than rigid categories that predetermine findings. Show how Suchman’s framework helps interpret an episode. Do not add theorists merely to increase theoretical density.

Review the research questions to reduce overlap while preserving their connection.
