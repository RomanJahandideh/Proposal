# Notes for talking with Rebecca

## 1. The idea in plain language (about 2 minutes)

> In my master's work I built systems that make a person's state visible to others: heart-rate cues for remote teammates, and a game world that reacts to the player. The strongest thing participants told me wasn't about speed or accuracy. It was about control. They wanted an off switch, a say in who sees what, and the right not to be read.
>
> For the PhD I want to keep the shared spaces and the responsive environments, but drop the bodily signals and look at AI. In a group improvisation, someone offers something, say a short phrase, and an AI turns it into part of a shared audiovisual scene. Then the interesting part starts. Did the group notice the offer? Does the person still feel it's theirs? Can they say "no, that's not what I meant", and does anyone listen? Who settles it, the person, the group or the facilitator? And who gets credit at the end?
>
> I'd build the environment in Unity so that the AI only *proposes* changes. People can accept, change, reject or undo them, and anyone can take part without AI. I'd also run sessions where a person does the transforming instead of the AI. That way I can tell what's specific to being reinterpreted by a machine and what's just being reinterpreted by someone else.
>
> I'd study this through interviews, a few co-design sessions and repeated workshops with small groups of adult improvisers, looking closely at video of those moments and asking participants to reflect on them. The contribution isn't the software. It's an account of how participation and authorship get recognised, or lost, when AI mediates collective creative work. I think it fits Technology in Practice and the research-creation option, and connects to your work on improvisation as a way of working with technology.

**If asked "why AI and not just rules?"** The question is about how people respond to a machine's *interpretation* of their material. Fixed rules don't interpret. The non-AI comparison is built in.

**If asked "what do you already have?"** Unity and C#, a working networked multiplayer system, a wearable-to-engine pipeline, two mixed-methods studies with ethics approval, and some experience calling LLM APIs with structured outputs. I'm not an ML researcher, and the design doesn't require one.

**If asked about the papers' status.** Both are submitted and under revision; neither is accepted. I can share the current drafts. I'm also fixing inconsistencies I found in the BioTerrarium paper before resubmitting it, so please don't rely on its numbers yet.
