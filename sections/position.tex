Architecture at Tabriz Islamic Art University and landscape architecture at
Istanbul Technical University taught me to treat designed environments as
media of social life; in ITU's Virtual Landscape Lab I studied how people
perceive mediated environments \citep{jahandideh2023}. My MA research in
Steve DiPaola's iViz Lab at Simon Fraser University asked what happens when
a technical system translates an ambiguous human state into a representation
shared with others. In a three-player networked Unity game, each role
carried an animated halo integrating heart rate and heart-rate variability,
offered as a trace to interpret rather than a verdict
\citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms}. Teams supported a struggling
teammate faster than with no cue; against an explicit numeric display the
difference in speed was not significant, the halo was rated less invasive,
and the numbers were rated more trustworthy and useful. Participants
repeatedly asked for optional, mutable visibility, with opt-in sharing and an
immediate off switch. \emph{BioTerrarium}, an ongoing physiologically
responsive Unity study, is designed so that physiology shapes fog,
vegetation, light and visual clarity in place of a meter, an approach I call
ambient reciprocity \citep{jahandideh_bioterrarium_ms}. Both projects treat
representation as shaping what others perceive and act on.

The PhD asks what happens when models and platforms translate participants'
words, images, sounds, gestures or cultural references into collective
artistic material. Neither MA study examined GenAI, collective authorship or
cultural production directly. They provide the conceptual problem of
mediated interpretation and the preparation, in Unity, C\#, networking,
real-time data, research ethics and mixed-methods studies, from which the
doctoral project develops toward artistic inquiry, participant observation
and platform analysis. As artist, developer, facilitator, observer, analyst
and curator, I will document how these roles shape what participants can do.
