I come to this question as a practitioner. Architecture at Tabriz Islamic
Art University and landscape architecture at Istanbul Technical University
taught me to treat designed environments as media of social life, in which
thresholds, paths and light shape who gathers and who is seen; in ITU's
Virtual Landscape Lab I studied how people perceive mediated environments
\citep{jahandideh2023}. My master's research in Interactive Arts and
Technology at Simon Fraser University, in Steve DiPaola's iViz Lab, asked
what happens when a technical system translates an ambiguous human state
into something others can perceive. In a three-player networked Unity game,
I rendered each teammate's heart rate and heart-rate variability as one
animated halo, designed on the premise that measurement constitutes rather
than reports what it names, so that the cue offered a trace to interpret
rather than an emotion label \citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms}. Teams supported
a struggling teammate faster than with no cue, matching an explicit numeric
display at lower workload and invasiveness, yet rated the numbers more
trustworthy and useful, so ambiguity and precision traded off. Participants accepted the cue only when it was optional,
mutable and subtle, and the paper argues for a right to be illegible. In
\emph{BioTerrarium}, a Unity world I built, the player's physiology shaped
fog, vegetation and light rather than a meter, an approach I called ambient
reciprocity \citep{jahandideh_bioterrarium_ms}. Across both projects,
representation was not neutral: it shaped what others could perceive, what
they trusted, when they offered help and what control people demanded over
their own visibility.

The doctorate carries that question from physiological signals to cultural
production. When a model and platform turn a participant's words, images,
sounds or gestures into collective material, whose interpretation becomes
visible, whose contribution remains recognizable, who can revise or refuse
the transformation, and who is credited or held responsible? Neither MA
study examined GenAI, authorship or culture; they gave me skills in Unity
and C\#, multiplayer systems, live-data integration and ethics-approved
mixed-methods research. The PhD moves from evaluating systems to artistic
inquiry, participant observation, focus groups and cultural analysis. As artist,
developer, facilitator, observer, analyst and curator, I will shape what
participants can do, so documenting my decisions is part of the method.
