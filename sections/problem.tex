GenAI now stands between many people and the means of cultural production. A
phrase becomes an image, a hummed line an arrangement, a sketch a rendered
scene. Each conversion is made by systems whose training data, defaults,
moderation rules and ownership are set elsewhere, largely by platform
companies, and are seldom open to the people whose contributions they
transform. I therefore treat GenAI not as a neutral tool or an autonomous
artist but as part of a cultural infrastructure that also includes
interfaces, access conditions, hidden labour, institutions, facilitators and
participants' own cultural knowledge \citep{crawford2021}.

Three connected issues give this infrastructure its stakes. First,
interpretive and creative authority is concentrating in opaque, proprietary
platforms. \citet[276]{richards2025} argues that the ``safe and helpful''
generative assistant functions ``as a proxy for authority'' that delineates
what its users can know and do. \citet[187--188,
194]{caines2026steaming} found that rising subscription costs, uneven
privacy tiers and opacity shaped what she and her students could make, and
\citet[267]{caines2023consultation} describe a project whose success or
failure lay ``completely in the hands of the corporations that designed the
software''. Second, GenAI can standardize and misrecognize culturally
situated expression. South Asian participants found that text-to-image
models reproduced an outsider's gaze on their cultures \citep{qadri2023}; AI
writing suggestions moved Indian writers toward Western styles
\citep{agarwal2025}; and stories written with model-generated ideas were
rated more creative individually but grew more alike collectively
\citep{doshi2024}. These findings concern particular models and tasks, not
GenAI as a whole. Third, creative labour is extracted unequally: image
generators are trained on artists' work without their consent, with
reported economic and reputational harm to those artists \citep{jiang2023}. Environmental cost, misinformation and job loss
form the wider context \citep[182]{caines2026steaming}, which this study does
not investigate empirically.

Participation and authorship matter because they are where these relations
become practical. They show who can make meaning with these systems,
whose contribution becomes visible, who can refuse a model's reading, who
receives credit and who answers for the work once it circulates publicly. One mechanism anchors the empirical work: a
system's technical record of who supplied what need not match how
collaborators recognize one another, so credit can come apart from influence
and responsibility. A small qualitative study cannot measure GenAI's effect
on society. Situated research-creation can make broad processes observable in
specific collaborative encounters and generate concepts that transfer
analytically, not statistically, to other settings.
