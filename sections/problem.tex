GenAI increasingly stands between people and the means of cultural
production, through systems whose training data, defaults, moderation rules
and ownership are set elsewhere, largely by platform companies
\citep{crawford2021}. I treat it not as a neutral tool or an autonomous
artist but as part of a cultural infrastructure that also includes
interfaces, access conditions, institutions and facilitators.

The study investigates three mechanisms through which this infrastructure
acquires social stakes. The first is \emph{platformized interpretive
authority}. \citet[276]{richards2025} argues that the ``safe and helpful''
assistant functions ``as a proxy for authority''; \citet[187--188,
194]{caines2026steaming} found that subscription costs, privacy tiers and
opacity shaped what she and her students could make; and
\citet[267]{caines2023consultation} describe a project whose outcome lay
``completely in the hands of the corporations that designed the software''.
The second is \emph{cultural legibility, standardization and
misrecognition}. South Asian participants found that text-to-image models
reproduced an outsider's gaze on their cultures \citep{qadri2023}, AI writing
suggestions moved Indian writers toward Western styles \citep{agarwal2025},
and stories written with model-generated ideas grew more alike collectively
\citep{doshi2024}; these findings concern particular models and tasks. The
third is \emph{conflict among technical provenance, recognition, authorship,
ownership, credit and responsibility}: a system can record who supplied what
while collaborators credit someone else, and artists' work enters training
data without their consent \citep{jiang2023}. Environmental cost, labour
displacement and misinformation are context \citep[182]{caines2026steaming},
not objects of this study.

Participation and authorship matter because they are where these mechanisms
become practical. They show who can make meaning with these systems, whose
contribution stays recognizable, who can refuse a model's reading, and who
answers for the work once it circulates. A small qualitative study cannot
measure GenAI's effect on society; situated research-creation can make these
processes observable and yield concepts that transfer analytically, not
statistically.
