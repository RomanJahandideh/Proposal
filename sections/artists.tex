My lineage is spatial and interactive rather than drawn from an AI-art
canon, but six practices shape this project's questions and decisions. Anna
Ridler's \emph{Myriad (Tulips)} (2018) exhibits thousands of tulip
photographs she took and labelled by hand, making the labour and subjective
classification inside a dataset into the artwork \citep{ridler2018}. It leads
me to treat prompts, labels and rejected outputs as exhibitable material; I
depart from it by making that labour collective and contested. Sougwen
Chung's \emph{Drawing Operations} pairs her drawing with robotic systems,
including one trained on her archived drawings and gestures
\citep{chung_dou}. It models co-creative performance between one artist and
a system shaped by her hand; my workshops involve many hands. Stephanie Dinkins's \emph{Not the Only One}
is trained on oral histories from three generations of women in her family
and hosted locally to protect community data \citep{dinkins_ntoo}. It makes
culturally situated data and its custody an artistic choice, which informs
my tool criteria. Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst's \emph{The Call} recorded
fifteen community choirs to train choral models and tested a data trust
between contributors and model users \citep{herndon2024call}. It is the
closest precedent for governing collective voice, but its governance was
designed by the artists and institution, whereas my workshops ask
participants to negotiate rules themselves. In Lauren Lee
McCarthy's \emph{LAUREN} the artist performs as a human smart-home
assistant, exposing the labour hidden inside automation
\citep{mccarthy_lauren}; as facilitator, I may be that hidden human. Caines's \emph{multiPLAY} explored digital community
engagement with Canadian improvisers through workshops, open studios and an
artist hackathon \citep{multiplay}, a model for improvised making across
media. Jennifer Walshe's insistence that ``AI is not a singular phenomenon''
\citep{walshe2023} underwrites the multi-tool design.
