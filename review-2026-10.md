My judgment: this is a credible exploratory PhD proposal, with a strong potential fit for York’s Communication & Culture program. I would revise it before sending it as your polished proposal to Rebecca. Keep the central idea; strengthen its cultural argument, comparative design, and intended contribution.

I read your full draft, Whose Contribution Counts? AI Mediation and Collective Authorship in Digital Improvisation. The comments below refer to its sections and pages.

What already works

Your strongest move is asking who has authority over a contribution after AI transforms it. That gives the project a communication-and-culture problem: recognition, authorship, interpretation, and power in collective creative practice.

The proposal also has a manageable technical approach, a plausible connection to Rebecca’s work, and an appropriate distinction between system logs and participants’ intentions. Keep those elements.

These are the changes I would make, in priority order.

1. Clarify which document you are preparing — page 1

York’s admissions application requests an approximately 500-word proposed plan of study, covering your research area, potential methods, program compatibility, and preparation. Your longer document is appropriate for discussion with Rebecca, but you need a separate application version.

The program’s published dissertation-proposal regulations describe an 8–10-page proposal plus bibliography, maximum 3,500 words, prepared later with a supervisory committee. These are different stages.

Also, remove the Lassonde logo from the header. It suggests an engineering submission, although this draft is for Communication & Culture. Use a simple, unbranded academic layout.

Shorten the status note to:

“Working proposal prepared for discussion with Dr. Rebecca Caines. The research setting and project partnerships will be developed in consultation with the prospective supervisor.”

Keep the specific access qualifications in Section 8.

2. Make the cultural stakes more explicit — Section 1

Your opening presents interesting questions, but it needs to explain why their answers matter beyond this particular workshop.

Add something like:

“When AI translates creative contributions into shared audiovisual material, its outputs may become more visible or influential than the contributions that prompted them. This raises a cultural question about whose interpretations gain authority, whose contributions remain recognisable, and how disagreement is negotiated in collective creative practice.”

Treat this as a possibility to investigate, not an established effect.

This sentence gives the project a stronger argument than “we need better acceptance and rejection controls.”

3. Narrow and substantiate the research gap — Section 2

Statements such as “research mostly models individual–system dyads” and “contestability research concerns decisions about people” are broad. A reviewer could challenge them with counterexamples.

Your acknowledgement of existing group-AI research is good. Build the gap around what those studies do not explain sufficiently, rather than how little research exists.

Suggested replacement:

“Existing research examines human–AI co-creation, collective decision-making, and attribution of creative credit. This project investigates their intersection in small-group digital improvisation: how a contribution becomes recognisable as someone’s work, how its transformation can be challenged, and how authority over the resulting artifact is negotiated over repeated sessions.”

Then compare your study with two or three closest studies: their participants, setting, methods, findings, and the specific question yours extends. A collection of citations alone does not demonstrate originality.

4. Broaden your definition of participation — Section 3

The sentence “I define participation by what happens to a contribution” is too restrictive.

A participant may contribute through listening, facilitating, supporting another person, or refusing to submit material. Your current definition risks excluding those practices.

Replace it with:

“I focus on the trajectory of identifiable creative contributions while also attending to listening, facilitation, silence, and refusal as forms of participation. Recognition will be examined through participants’ interpretations and interactions, rather than inferred solely from incorporation into the artifact.”

Also define authorship here: are you studying felt ownership, public attribution, creative responsibility, or legal ownership? I recommend focusing on the first three and explicitly excluding legal copyright determination.

5. Repair the comparisons — Section 6

This is the most important methodological issue.

Your arrangements change two things:

Arrangement A:
Who transforms material? AI.
Who approves it? Contributor.

Arrangement B:
Who transforms material? AI.
Who approves it? Group.

Arrangement C:
Who transforms material? Human.
Who approves it? Currently unclear.

Consequently, arrangement C does not automatically “separate” human transformation from model transformation. It may also change approval authority, timing, social relationships, and expectations.

Replace that claim with:

“The contributor-led and group-led arrangements explore different distributions of approval authority. A human-mediated arrangement, using comparable assets and approval rules, offers an additional interpretive contrast. These workshops will support situated comparison rather than isolate a causal effect of AI.”

Specify who approves material in C. You can retain exploratory comparisons, but your conclusions must match what they can establish.

6. Explain how analysis produces doctoral knowledge — Section 9

The methods are plausible, but currently read as a list: recordings, logs, interaction analysis, thematic analysis.

Add a concrete analytical procedure:

“I will construct multimodal accounts of selected episodes, linking participants’ words and actions with screen events and system records. Analysis will compare accepted transformations, contested transformations, refusals, and episodes in which participants disagree about authorship. Subsequent interviews will provide participants’ interpretations, including accounts that challenge my initial reading. Comparisons across sessions will examine how these practices change as groups establish shared norms.”

Explain that theoretical concepts guide attention without predetermining the findings. Avoid implying that a provenance record establishes the “true” interpretation against which participants are judged.

7. Strengthen the research-creation component — Sections 6–7

At present, the creative work could be read as a research instrument built to collect workshop data.

Explain what participants actually create: a collective audiovisual improvisation, a performed environment, or another clearly specified form. What makes it a creative practice rather than a sequence of interface tasks?

Add:

“The creative component will comprise the shared audiovisual environment and the improvised works produced through it. Developing and revising its expressive possibilities, interaction rules, and workshop scores will form part of the inquiry into how creative authority is distributed.”

Give one short example of a session. That will make the proposal easier to understand without adding more implementation detail.

8. Reframe why you use AI — Section 7

“AI is needed” and “a fixed mapping would not produce open readings” overstate the argument. Human interpretation and other generative techniques can also produce ambiguity.

Use:

“Generative AI is included because machine-mediated reinterpretation is the object of study. The project does not presume that AI improves improvisation; it examines how participants respond to its transformations and negotiate their place in the shared work.”

Also, call the visible one-line output a generated interpretation, not an explanation of the model’s reasoning. It cannot establish why the model actually produced a particular transformation.

Move some engineering details—timeouts, command patterns, clamping—into a short feasibility paragraph or appendix.

9. Correct the withdrawal promise — Section 10

This sentence needs changing:

“Provenance lets individuals withdraw their own contributions from collective artifacts.”

Tracking a contribution does not guarantee that you can remove all its influence from a collectively developed work.

Replace it with:

“Provenance will support identifying and, where feasible, removing attributable contributions. Participants will agree on withdrawal procedures and deadlines before workshops, including the limits of removing contributions from jointly developed or publicly disseminated works.”

Similarly, “full participation is possible without AI” should be a design commitment you test. Name practical provisions such as reduced-motion settings, readable text, alternatives to sound-only information, and facilitator-assisted controls.

10. State a sharper scholarly contribution — Section 11

“A scholarly account,” “an analytical framework,” and “design implications” are reasonable categories, but too general.

Suggested replacement:

“The intended scholarly contribution is an account of how creative authority is negotiated across contributors, facilitators, interface rules, and AI-generated transformations. The study will examine when technical attribution supports recognition and when it fails to resolve disagreement about authorship. The creative system and workshop documentation will make these tensions available for analysis and further practice.”

Finding that AI is unnecessary is not automatically a doctoral contribution. Explaining when, how, and why it becomes unnecessary—and what that reveals about collective creativity—could be.

11. Strengthen your preparation and Rebecca connection — Sections 5 and 14

Your master’s research is a useful bridge. However, retain the claims about participants requesting control and hidden evaluative categories only if your manuscript or documented analysis supports them. Distinguish findings from your reflections as a designer.

Add one concise sentence connecting your architecture, landscape architecture, spatial interaction, HCI, and independent system-development experience. York specifically asks applicants to explain their preparation when their previous degrees appear distant from the program.

Rebecca’s 2026 generative-AI publication is real: its publisher describes research-creation involving interdisciplinary education, ethics, access, collaboration, and improvisatory skills. Your connection is valid, but explain your extension: your study examines contribution-level recognition and contested authorship in repeated group improvisation. Do not imply that her funded project already contains your proposed AI system.

My recommendation is to keep this direction and title. Revise the methods and contribution first, then reduce the defensive wording and technical detail. The central proposal should leave Rebecca with a clear understanding of what cultural problem you will investigate, how the creative practice generates evidence, and what your dissertation could explain that existing work does not yet explain adequately.
