# Related-work evidence matrix and table-design notes

Prepared 2026-10-11 for `proposal-rebecca.tex` (second review, comment set for the comparison table and schematic). Labels used below:

- **Verified:** read in the source named.
- **Abstract only:** only the abstract was accessible; no other detail is inferred.
- **Interpretation:** my reading.

"Not reported" means the source does not report it. It does not mean the thing was absent.

**Design decisions for the proposal table (interpretation):**
- Rows are studies, not systems.
- The columns combine the most relevant factors: individual, dyadic or group practice; AI role; who interprets, approves and credits; technical vs social recognition; facilitator and designer influence; duration; evidence type.
- There are no ticks or crosses.
- Each row's final cell states an extension, not a deficit.
- I did not reuse Zhang's or Rezwana's categories as column headings. Where a concept is borrowed (e.g. "locus" of authority), it is credited in the prose.
- York does not require a comparison table; this is a choice made for readability.

**Dissertations (positioning, not table rows):**
- **Neville (2026).** Builds a concept (authoritarian diffusion) from a concrete case and a wider cultural problem: communities whose techniques are not recognised as technical. *Lesson applied:* the proposal's ownership question treats improvisers' conventions as existing practices that a system can override.
- **Richards (2025).** States an explicit argument about AI and authority (that "safe" models extend counterinsurgent governance). *Lesson applied:* the contribution is stated as a proposition, that attribution and recognition may diverge.
- **Chalk (2022).** Iterative recruitment adjusted to gaps, transferability rather than generalisability, and named sample gaps (pp. 52–56). *Lesson applied:* the recruitment and reporting plan.
- None is a direct empirical comparison, so none is a table row.

## Part C. Originality test

- **Tested contribution:** "The study follows how identifiable contributions acquire recognition, authority, and authorship through AI-mediated transformations in repeated collective improvisation, examining whether technical attribution and social recognition diverge."
- **What already exists:**
  - group GenAI use and negotiation (Xu);
  - AI shaping social roles in co-creation (Suh, abstract);
  - improvisation practice studied to design AI (Trajkova);
  - AI as an improvising performer (Mathewson & Mirowski);
  - lay attribution of credit for AI art (Epstein);
  - unacknowledged contributions as a group breakdown (Xu, code C2).
- **Narrowed claim:** the combination is the extension. It brings together repeated group improvisation; a mediator that transforms one person's offer for others; explicit approval arrangements, including a human-mediated contrast; facilitation; and comparison of technical attribution with socially negotiated recognition over time.
- **Not claimed:** first group-AI creative system; first study of AI authorship or improvisation; fairer participation; any demonstrated benefit.
- **Change flagged:** Xu's C2 code ("unacknowledged contributions") led me to drop any implication that recognition in AI-supported groups is unstudied.
