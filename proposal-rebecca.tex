\section{Problem and significance}

Generative AI is entering collaborative creative tools, where a model does
more than produce content: it \emph{reinterprets} what people contribute. One
person offers a phrase, the system turns it into something else, and a group
must decide what to make of it. Was the contribution acknowledged? Does the
transformation still belong to its contributor? Who may object, who settles
disagreement, and who is credited? These are questions about how participation
and authorship are recognised in collective cultural production, and about how
model defaults and interface permissions shape whose expression counts.
Improvisation is a strong setting for studying them: improvisers already have
practices for accepting, building on and refusing offers, and critical
improvisation studies treats co-creation as an ethical, rights-bearing relation
\citep{fischlin2013,caines2021fragile}. A machine that transforms offers
changes that relation in observable ways.

\section{Research gap}

Human--AI co-creation research mostly models individual--system dyads and
their control mechanisms \citep{horvitz1999,guzdial2019,rezwana2023,zhang2025agency};
AI in improvisation is usually a partner or performer
\citep{mathewson2017,mirowski2023,trajkova2024}. Group use of generative AI is
an active topic: \citet{xu2026} found shared GenAI output became ``negotiable
contributions to group decisions'' in student teamwork, so ``groups plus AI''
is not itself a gap. Credit studies are largely experimental or individual
\citep{epstein2020,draxler2024}, and contestability research concerns
decisions about people rather than creative reinterpretations of their work
\citep{hirsch2017,lyons2021}. What remains under-explained is how a
\emph{particular person's} contribution comes to count once a model has
reinterpreted it inside a group, and how facilitators, interface permissions
and model defaults \citep{bianchi2023} jointly configure who can acknowledge,
contest and credit it.

\section{Research questions}

\textbf{Central question.} In collaborative digital improvisation, how do
participants, facilitators and a generative-AI interface jointly determine
whether and how a person's contribution is acknowledged, transformed, taken
up, contested and credited in the shared work?

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
  \item How do participants interpret differences between what they intended
        and how the model transformed their contribution?
  \item How do groups and facilitators negotiate acceptance, revision,
        disagreement and refusal, and who comes to have standing to decide?
  \item How do these negotiations shape participants' recognition of
        authorship and responsibility for the resulting work, and how does
        this compare with an arrangement in which a person, rather than a
        model, transforms the material?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent I define \emph{participation} by what happens to a contribution,
not by speaking time or click counts: is it acknowledged, transformed and
incorporated; can its contributor contest the transformation; who resolves
disagreement; who is recognised? I do not assume that voting is fair, that
individual control suffices, or that facilitation is harmful. A majority can
override someone, and a facilitator can help a quieter person take part; these
tensions are what the study examines.

\section{Theoretical framework}

My primary framework is Lucy Suchman's account of human--machine
reconfiguration \citep{suchman2007}: agency, including authorship, is not a
fixed property of people or machines but is \emph{attributed} in situated
practice. Three concepts guide the data:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Attribution:} how participants assign agency and authorship
        in talk and action (``the AI made that'', ``that one's mine''),
        compared with the provenance log.
  \item \textbf{Configuration} \citep[with][]{woolgar1990}: how interface
        permissions, model defaults, facilitation and my own design decisions
        make some actions easy and others hard.
  \item \textbf{Located accountability:} who is treated as responsible when a
        transformation misrepresents someone.
\end{itemize}

A limited complementary perspective from critical improvisation studies adds
two ideas: \emph{uptake}, meaning whether an offer is heard and built on, and
co-creation as an ethical relation \citep{fischlin2013,sawyer2009}. I chose
Suchman over Actor-Network Theory, which recent ComCult dissertations used
productively \citep{mcarthur2015,chalk2022}, because her focus on how agency
is attributed, and on the designer's own accountability, maps directly onto
the authorship question.

\section{Relationship to my master's research}

My SFU master's work asked how interfaces make a person's state perceptible
to others. In a cooperative Unity game I rendered remote teammates' heart rate
and HRV as label-free ambient icons (manuscript submitted to an ACM venue;
revisions requested). A second project built a Unity environment that responds
to heart rate (submitted to ACM SIGGRAPH 2026; revisions requested; I do not
rely on its results here). The most important lesson was not about speed:
participants made acceptance conditional on control (an off switch, separate
permissions), and the classification scheme carried evaluative categories that
participants never saw. Both point to one question: \emph{who controls how a
shared system interprets people?} The PhD keeps my strengths in shared spaces,
ambient and spatial communication, participant interpretation and system
building. It moves from involuntary bodily signals, which I do not claim reveal
emotion \citep{barrett2019,stark2021}, to deliberate creative contributions,
and from consent to \emph{visibility} to the contestation of
\emph{transformation}.

\section{Research-creation methodology}

The project follows the Joint Program's research-creation option: a creative
work plus a 150--200-page written dissertation that carries the argument
\citep{comcult_rc}. I approach it as ``research-from-creation''
\citep{chapman2012}: the system and workshops are where the evidence is
generated, not ends in themselves. My design decisions are part of what I
study \citep{khaled2018,loveless2019}. The project has four phases:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Phase \arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Scoping interviews} with improvisers and facilitators about
        how offers, refusal and credit currently work. These shape the
        workshop scores and the system's permissions.
  \item \textbf{Co-design} sessions with a few practitioners to choose the
        input form, bound the transformations and design refusal and credit
        controls.
  \item \textbf{Repeated workshops} with two or three small groups (three to
        five people), each meeting several times so that norms can develop,
        under three arrangements: (a) the contributor decides on
        transformations of their own material; (b) the group decides; (c) a
        person, not a model, transforms material with the same tools. These
        are comparative probes, not experimental conditions; (c) separates
        being transformed by someone from being transformed by a model.
  \item \textbf{Reflection interviews} in which participants review video
        episodes of their own sessions.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent These numbers are provisional and depend on access and on when new
kinds of episode stop appearing; a small qualitative study does not
generalise statistically.

\section{Proposed system and the bounded role of AI}

The creative work is a shared Unity audiovisual environment for small-group
improvisation. A participant submits a short text phrase (sketches may follow
if co-design supports them). An existing language model returns \emph{one
proposal}: a schema-checked object selecting from a closed set of Unity
actions over a curated library of forms, light, colour, particle ``weather'',
sound and text, with clamped parameters, plus a visible one-line ``reading''
of the phrase.

\textbf{Why AI is needed.} The question concerns responses to a machine's
\emph{interpretation} of creative material; a fixed mapping would not produce
the open readings participants must interpret, accept or refuse. The non-AI
arrangement provides the contrast. \textbf{The model cannot decide} whether
anything enters the work, who is credited, what is removed, or anything about
participants; there is no emotion or behaviour inference.

\textbf{Controls.} Depending on the arrangement, contributors or groups can
inspect, edit, accept, reject, request another reading or undo. A manual,
non-AI path to the same library is always available.

\textbf{Safe execution.} Model output is never executed as code: Unity
applies only allow-listed commands with clamped parameters, through an
undoable command pattern.
Invalid output, unknown assets, timeouts or service failures discard the
proposal, the manual path stays open, and facilitators can switch AI off.

\textbf{Provenance.} Each element records a contributor pseudonym, the
original phrase, model and prompt version, the proposal, and each decision with
who made it and when. Logs record system events, not intentions. Whether credit
is displayed is a session setting, since visible attribution can also expose
people.

\textbf{Model and cost.} I will first test a locally run open-weight model,
avoiding third-party processing; otherwise, a hosted API under institutionally
approved, no-training terms, sending only the phrase. Text-only requests are
inexpensive but will be budgeted. No foundation-model training: my experience
is applied tool-building with LLM APIs, not machine-learning research.

\section{Case-study context and access}

\textbf{Primary context (subject to Dr.\ Caines's permission).} Workshops with
adult improvising artists and creative-technology practitioners in Toronto, run
as a student sub-study within or alongside her SSHRC Insight project
\emph{Interfacing, Playtesting, Resetting: Reframing Technology Use Through
Improvised Creative Technologies} \citep{yfile2026sshrc}. Only that project's
title and funding are public. I do not assume its methods, participants or any
AI component.

\textbf{Alternative.} Independent open calls to Toronto improvisation, theatre
and sound communities and to AMPD students (excluding anyone I teach), with a
host arts organisation only if one agrees. Historical projects such as
multiPLAY are precedents, not recruitment sources. I will not work with
disability-focused or Indigenous communities without established partnerships,
community governance and relevant expertise.

\section{Data collection and analysis}

The data are interview and session audio and video, screen capture, system
logs, artifacts and my design journal. I will identify \emph{episodes} that
run from a contribution, through its transformation, the group's
interpretation and response (acceptance, revision, disagreement or refusal),
to the next creative action. I will analyse them through microethnographic
interaction analysis \citep{jordan1995,mcarthur2015} and read them alongside
participants' own reflections, using reflexive thematic analysis
\citep{braun2019} for the interviews. Suchman's three concepts organise the
coding. As McArthur found, observation and self-report may diverge, and those
divergences count as findings.

\section{Ethics, accessibility and reflexivity}

Consent will be separate and ongoing for recording, for AI processing, and
for public dissemination \citep{im2021,strengers2021}. Provenance lets
individuals withdraw their own contributions from collective artifacts; credit
for public works is agreed with participants; time is compensated; data are
minimised, pseudonymised and retained for defined periods, with any third-party
processing disclosed. Model defaults are discussed openly with participants
\citep{bianchi2023}, full participation is possible without AI, and power
differences, including the facilitator's, are examined rather than assumed
away. I make no claims of wellbeing benefit, and consent forms do not replace
community governance \citep{birhane2022}. As designer, researcher and possibly
facilitator I shape what I study, so I will keep a design journal and, where
possible, work with a co-facilitator.

\section{Expected contributions}

These are \emph{intended} outputs, not findings:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a scholarly account of how AI mediation shapes recognition,
        contestation and authorship in collective improvisation;
  \item if the data justify it, an analytical framework for the ``life'' of a
        contribution;
  \item a documented creative research system, together with consented
        workshop works;
  \item design implications.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The study may find that AI is unnecessary in some arrangements, or
that refusal becomes a valued creative practice. Either result would be a
contribution.

\section{Fit with the program and supervision}

The project sits in \textbf{Technology in Practice}, with a minor in
\textbf{Media \& Culture} or a constructed minor in HCI if the program approves
one. It studies technology as a cultural practice of participation and
authorship, which matches the program's commitment to applying critical theory
to contemporary communication practices. Dr.\ Caines's work on improvisation
as a methodology for human--technology interaction, socially responsive
creative technologies \citep{caines2023consultation}, and her recent
research-creation study of generative AI as ``a kind of critical improvisation
practice'' \citep{caines2026steaming} make her expertise directly relevant to
the setting, the methods and the creative component.
