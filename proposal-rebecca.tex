\section{Problem and cultural stakes}

Generative models now sit inside tools that people use to make things
together, and in these tools a model does more than produce content: it
\emph{reinterprets} what people contribute. Someone types a phrase, and a
moment later it has become a sound or a moving form in a shared scene. The
group then has to decide what that transformation means, and whether it
still belongs to the person who offered it.

The stakes reach beyond any single workshop. When AI translates creative
contributions into shared audiovisual material, its outputs may become more
visible or influential than the contributions that prompted them. I treat
this as a possibility to investigate, not an established effect. If it does
happen, it raises a cultural question about whose interpretations gain
authority, whose contributions remain recognisable, and how disagreement is
negotiated in collective creative practice. It also raises a question about
the tools themselves. Creative platforms increasingly record who supplied
what, and it is tempting to assume that such records settle questions of
credit. This project asks whether they do.

Improvisation is a good place to ask, because improvisers already have ways
of listening to, building on and refusing one another's offers. Improvisation
has also been argued to be a research method in its own right, one grounded
in risk, active listening and collaborative response
\citep{caines2020fragile}. These are established practices with their own
conventions about what an offer is and who may use it. A machine that
transforms offers, and an interface that labels them, enter these practices
rather than an empty space, and the people involved have to work out where
the machine fits.

\section{Research gap}

Existing research examines human--AI co-creation, collective decision-making
and the attribution of creative credit. This project investigates their
intersection in small-group digital improvisation: how a contribution becomes
recognisable as someone's work, how its transformation can be challenged, and
how authority over the resulting artifact is negotiated over repeated
sessions. Three studies come closest to this question, and each leaves a
different part of it open.

\citet{xu2026} observed six groups of three university students designing a
cultural educational game around one shared ChatGPT interface, in a single
45-minute session, and interviewed nine further students. Shared AI output
became an anchor for attention, a shared memory and a negotiable part of the
groups' decisions. The authors note that their analysis emphasised
information flow over social aspects such as trust and accountability. I take
their question into creative work in which a specific person's material is
transformed, and I follow authorship and accountability across repeated
sessions.

\citet{trajkova2024} ran focus groups with 24 dance students about partnered
movement improvisation, in order to design an AI dance partner. In that work
the AI is a \emph{partner}. In mine it is a \emph{mediator}, reinterpreting
one person's contribution for a whole group.

\citet{epstein2020} showed, through online vignette experiments, that
describing an AI as an agent rather than a tool shifts the credit and
responsibility that observers assign to the people behind an AI artwork.
Their participants judged a finished work from the outside. I ask how credit
and responsibility are claimed and contested \emph{in situ}, by the people
making the work.

Questions of contestation point the same way. Contestability has mainly been
developed for high-stakes algorithmic decisions such as hiring or sentencing
\citep{lyons2021}. Much less has been said about what it means to challenge a
machine's \emph{creative} reading of one's contribution, in front of
collaborators. This literature base is deliberately narrow for a discussion
draft. Before the formal proposal I will extend it into scholarship on
collective authorship and creative labour in communication and cultural
studies.

\section{Key terms and research questions}

Three working definitions organise the study. \textbf{Recognition}: a
contribution is noticed or acknowledged as meaningful. \textbf{Creative
authority}: a person's interpretation or decision carries influence over what
happens next. \textbf{Authorship}: participants associate ownership, credit or
responsibility with the work. I study authorship in three registers: felt
ownership, public attribution and creative responsibility. Legal ownership
and copyright are outside the scope of this study.

The three can come apart. A contributor may be credited on screen while the
group reshapes the material without regard to their view, so they have
credit without influence. A facilitator, an interpreter, or I as the designer
of the asset library may shape the work decisively without being credited, so
we have influence without credit. I use these definitions as sensitising
concepts. They direct attention without fixing in advance which categories
the participants themselves will use.

Participation is broader than submitting material. I focus on the trajectory
of identifiable creative contributions, while also attending to listening,
facilitation, silence and refusal as forms of participation. Recognition will
be examined through participants' interpretations and interactions, rather
than inferred solely from incorporation into the artifact. I do not assume
that group decision-making is fair, that individual control is enough, or
that facilitation does harm. A majority can override someone; a facilitator
can help a quieter participant take part.

\textbf{Central question.} In collaborative digital improvisation, how do
participants, facilitators and a generative-AI interface shape whether a
person's contribution is recognised, carries creative authority, and is
credited in the shared work?

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Recognition.} How do participants notice, interpret and respond
        to differences between what they offered and how it was transformed?
  \item \emph{Authority.} Through which practices, including acceptance,
        revision, refusal, facilitation and silence, do contributors and
        groups gain or lose influence over transformed material, and how do
        interface permissions shape those practices?
  \item \emph{Authorship.} When and why do the system's technical attribution
        and participants' own accounts of ownership, credit and responsibility
        diverge?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical framework}

My primary framework is Lucy Suchman's study of human--machine
reconfiguration \citep{suchman2007}, which examines how agencies are figured
at the interface and how humans and machines come to be treated as similar or
different in practice. Read this way, the question of whether a transformed
element ``belongs'' to its contributor, to the group or to the system is not
settled in advance. People work it out as they interact, and the interface,
the model and the people who designed them all take part.

Take the illustrative episode described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. The system's record
says that the amber pulse came from one participant's phrase. Yet the pulse
also depends on the model's reading, on the asset library I built and on the
approval rule in force. When the contributor refuses another participant's
request to brighten it, the contributor is enacting authority that the
interface has granted, and the group can accept or contest that authority.
Through Suchman's lens, the question shifts from ``who really made the
pulse?'' to how, in this moment, agency is distributed and attributed across
people and machine.

This reading gives me three questions to bring to the material:
\begin{itemize}
  \item how participants attribute agency and authorship in talk and action
        (``the AI did that'', ``that one is mine'');
  \item how interface rules, model defaults, facilitation and my own design
        decisions make some actions easy and others difficult;
  \item who is held responsible when a transformation misrepresents someone.
\end{itemize}

Improvisation studies adds a limited, complementary lens: attention to
listening and response, and to how an offer is taken up, ignored or refused
\citep{caines2020fragile}. These concepts tell me where to look. They do not
decide what I will find.

\section{Research-creation methodology}\label{sec:method}

The project follows the program's research-creation option, which combines a
creative work with a written dissertation of 150--200 pages that develops the
argument \citep{comcult_rc}. Of the modes \citeauthor{chapman2012}
\citeyearpar{chapman2012} describe, it is closest to
``research-from-creation'': making the work generates material for
understanding how participants engage with it.

\subsection*{The creative work}

The creative component will comprise the shared audiovisual environment and
the improvised works produced through it. Developing and revising its
expressive possibilities, interaction rules and workshop scores will form
part of the inquiry into how creative authority is distributed. What
participants make are short collective audiovisual improvisations: projected,
spatial scenes of light, form and sound that they build and perform together
in real time, and then talk about. The work produces knowledge as well as
data. Every rule it embodies, such as who may approve, what is labelled, and
which assets exist, is a proposition about collective authorship that
participants can test, resist or rewrite.

A brief, illustrative session shows what this looks like. Four improvisers
receive a score: build the weather of a city none of them has visited. One
types ``the bus that never comes.'' The system proposes a slow amber pulse at
the edge of the space with a low drone, and displays its generated
interpretation, ``waiting as a distant repeating light.'' The contributor
accepts it. Later, another participant wants the pulse brighter. The
contributor declines, and the facilitator asks the group how to proceed.
Moments like these, of acceptance, challenge and resolution, are what the
study examines.

The research-creation guidelines require the creative work to be available
in some form so that the examining committee can assess its contribution
\citep{comcult_rc}. The creative component will be documented through the
executable environment, workshop scores, selected consented recordings, and
an annotated account of design revisions. These materials will allow the
committee to examine how the creative process contributes to the
dissertation's argument. A public sharing is possible but not essential.
Consent for research use, for examination, and for public dissemination will
be sought separately.

\subsection*{Does the system manufacture ownership?}

The interface records where each contribution came from and, in some
arrangements, gives its contributor the right to approve changes. These
choices could encourage a sense of individual ownership even where
improvisers usually treat offers as material for everyone. Several questions
are therefore open:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Do practitioners ordinarily credit individual offers?
  \item Does accepting an offer make it available to the group?
  \item May others build on accepted material without asking again?
  \item Who decides when an element has become collective?
  \item Does visible attribution change existing conventions?
\end{itemize}
Scoping interviews will investigate how practitioners already understand
ownership and reuse of improvised offers. Co-design will examine whether
contributor approval and visible attribution support their practices or
introduce individualised ownership assumptions that participants do not
share. The approval rules and attribution display described below are
provisional and will be revised after scoping. One option to test is a way
for accepted material to be ``released'' to the group.

\subsection*{Phases and provisional scale}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Scoping interviews} with improvisers and facilitators about how
        offers, credit, reuse and refusal work in their practice, with and
        without technology.
  \item \emph{Co-design} sessions with a few practitioners to choose the input
        form, bound the transformations, revise the approval and attribution
        rules, and test accessibility provisions. Co-design participants will
        not form the workshop groups. This keeps the people who shaped the
        rules separate from those encountering them, though they may be
        invited to view the final work.
  \item \emph{Repeated workshops.} A provisional design would involve three
        groups of three to five adult practitioners, each attending four
        workshops of approximately 90--120 minutes. Recruitment would seek
        variation in improvisational and creative-technology experience. The
        final design would be refined through scoping, access negotiations
        and ethics review.
  \item \emph{Reflection interviews} with each participant after the final
        workshop, reviewing video episodes from their own sessions.
\end{enumerate}

These numbers are planning figures, not confirmed access. Three groups allow
comparison across different group compositions, and four sessions give each
group time to develop norms. I would aim for one existing ensemble and
groups formed for the study, and record participants' prior relationships,
since familiarity is likely to affect how freely people refuse one another.

\subsection*{Arrangements}

The first workshop introduces the environment through the manual,
non-AI path. In it the group agrees, and writes down, the procedure it will
use when the group decides. Each later workshop uses one arrangement, and
the three groups meet the arrangements in different orders:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{0.92\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.3cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Arrangement & Who proposes the transformation & Who approves it \\
\midrule
Contributor-led & AI system & The contributor \\
Group-led & AI system & The group, by the procedure it agreed in the first workshop \\
Human-mediated & Another participant in a rotating ``interpreter'' role, using the same asset library & The contributor (same rule as contributor-led) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The contributor-led and group-led arrangements explore different
distributions of approval authority. The human-mediated arrangement, which
uses comparable assets and the same approval rule as the contributor-led one,
offers an additional interpretive contrast. It also changes timing, social
relationships and expectations. These workshops will therefore support
situated comparison rather than isolate a causal effect of AI.

Varying the order does not remove carry-over. A group that has learned to
refuse in one arrangement may refuse more readily in the next, and a
procedure agreed early may harden into a norm. I will treat these
developments as evidence about how conventions of authority form. Any change
a group makes to its procedure is noted on the written procedure and in the
system record. The facilitator's interventions, whether prompting, mediating
or stepping back, are recorded in session notes and visible in video. When I
facilitate, a co-facilitator will take notes where possible.

\section{The system and the place of AI}

A participant submits a short text phrase; sketches may be added later if
co-design supports them. An existing language model returns one
\emph{proposal}, a choice of elements and settings from a curated library of
forms, light, colour, particle ``weather'', sound and text, together with a
one-line \emph{generated interpretation} of the phrase that everyone can
see. That line is part of the model's output. It does not explain why the
model produced the transformation, which it cannot establish. Depending on
the arrangement, contributors or groups can inspect, edit, accept, reject,
request another proposal or undo. A manual path to the same library is always
open, so no one needs AI in order to contribute.

Generative AI is included because machine-mediated reinterpretation is the
object of study. The project does not presume that AI improves improvisation;
it examines how participants respond to its transformations and negotiate
their place in the shared work. The model does not decide whether anything
enters the work, who is credited or what is removed, and it makes no
inferences about participants' emotions or behaviour.

Neither the model nor the library is a neutral container. Research on
generative models raises concerns about stereotyped representation
\citep{bianchi2023}. For language models specifically, several widely used
models have been shown to reflect cultural values resembling English-speaking
and Protestant European countries \citep{tao2024}, and multilingual and
Arabic language models have been shown to favour Western-associated entities
\citep{naous2024}. Language models also still fall short of human
performance in interpreting figurative phrases \citep{liu2022}. Because this
system uses a language model and a curated audiovisual library, I will
examine how both model interpretations and my asset choices privilege
particular associations. Participants may write in metaphor, idiom or
languages other than English. I will record how such contributions are
read, and discuss misreadings with the people who made them. Any conclusions
will concern the people and practices in the study, not cultures or
communities it does not include.

\textbf{Feasibility.} The technical design is deliberately modest, so that
effort goes into the expressive and social rules the study examines. Unity
applies only a fixed set of permitted actions with bounded settings. Model
output is checked against that set and never run as code; malformed output
or a service failure simply produces no proposal. Each proposal and decision
is recorded and can be undone. I will first test a locally run open-weight
model, which avoids third-party processing, and otherwise use a hosted
service under institutionally approved terms, sending only the phrase. The
project uses existing models without training or fine-tuning them.

\section{Case-study context and access}

\textbf{Possible primary context.} Subject to discussion with Dr.\ Caines,
these workshops could examine how improvisational practices help
participants question and reshape the authority built into creative
technologies. That question sits close to the title of her SSHRC Insight
project, \emph{Interfacing, Playtesting, Resetting: Reframing Technology Use
Through Improvised Creative Technologies} \citep{yfile2026sshrc}. The
particular setting and relationship to her funded project would be
determined collaboratively. Only the project's title and funding are public,
and I do not assume that it includes this study, these participants or any
AI component.

\textbf{Alternative.} If that connection is not appropriate, I would
recruit through open calls to Toronto improvisation, theatre and sound
communities and to AMPD students, excluding anyone I teach, with an arts
organisation as host only if it agrees. Like Chalk's study of Actual Play
producers \citep{chalk2022}, recruitment would be iterative. After the first
group I would check whose practices are missing and adjust later calls,
reporting the gaps that remain. Earlier projects such as multiPLAY are
precedents for digitally mediated improvisation, not recruitment sources. I
will not work with disability-focused or Indigenous communities without
established partnerships, community governance and relevant expertise.

\section{Analysis}

I will construct multimodal accounts of selected episodes. Each account
links video of participants' words and actions with the screen recording and
the system's log of proposals and decisions. An episode runs from a
contribution, through its transformation and the group's response, to the
next creative action. Episode selection will include routine acceptance and
non-response as well as explicit disagreement. I will document selection
decisions and compare episodes across groups, arrangements, and stages of
collaboration, including cases that challenge emerging interpretations.

For the episodes I adapt the video microethnography that \citet{mcarthur2015}
used to study players' interactions with interfaces. Reflection interviews
will be analysed through reflexive thematic analysis \citep{braun2019}. The
themes contextualise the episode accounts by showing how participants
describe ownership and authority in their own terms, but they do not settle
what an episode meant. System records show what happened in the software;
they are not a ``true'' account of intention against which participants are
judged.

Interpretation moves between three comparisons. Within an episode, I set
the record, the interaction and the participant's later account side by
side. Across arrangements, I ask whether authority behaves differently when
the group or a human interpreter is involved. Across sessions, I look at how
conventions form. Discrepant cases are the main check on my reading. One
example would be a group that ignores the attribution display entirely.
Such a case either revises an emerging explanation or limits its scope. In
keeping with a reflexive approach, interpretation will not be validated by
inter-rater agreement. Instead, I will keep an analytic trail. Alongside it,
the design journal records how my decisions as designer and facilitator
shape what participants can do, so that this influence can be examined
rather than hidden.

\section{Ethics, consent and accessibility}

Consent will be specific, ongoing and revisable
\citep{im2021,strengers2021}. Participants consent separately to recording,
to AI processing of their contributions, to use of recordings in the
examination of the creative work, and to public dissemination.

Withdrawal needs particular care in collective work. Provenance will support
identifying and, where feasible, removing attributable contributions.
Participants will agree on withdrawal procedures and deadlines before the
workshops, including the limits of removing contributions from jointly
developed or publicly disseminated works. Credit for public works will be
agreed with participants, and their time will be paid. Data will be
minimised and pseudonymised, any third-party processing disclosed, and
retention periods stated.

Full participation without AI is a design commitment that the workshops will
test. Planned provisions are a reduced-motion setting, readable and resizable
text, visual or textual alternatives to sound-only information, controls that
a facilitator can operate on a participant's behalf, and the manual path.
Participation alone does not make a project equitable \citep{birhane2022}.
As designer, researcher and possibly facilitator, I shape the situation I
study. The design journal and, where possible, a co-facilitator help keep
that influence visible.

\section{Intended contribution}

The project investigates whether technical attribution and social
recognition can diverge in collective creative work. A provenance log may
record who supplied a phrase while collaborators treat the transformed
material as belonging to another person, to the group, or to nobody in
particular. The study will examine how contributors gain or lose authority
over transformed material, and how interface permissions, facilitation and
group conventions shape that process. Its intended contribution is an
explanation of these relationships, rather than a general claim that more
individual control produces fairer collaboration.

This is a proposition to investigate, not a finding. RQ3 asks directly
whether and when the divergence occurs. RQ1 and RQ2 trace the recognition
and authority that would produce it. The episode comparisons and reflection
interviews provide the evidence. If the divergence appears, the dissertation
would develop an account of it that reaches beyond these workshops: to
creative platforms that rely on attribution records, and to the improvised
conventions that such records meet. If it does not appear, the study would
explain why attribution and recognition stayed aligned in these settings.
Similarly, if AI turns out to be unnecessary in some arrangements, the
contribution would lie in explaining when, how and why, and what that
reveals about collective creativity. The documented creative work makes
these tensions available for examination and for further practice.

\section{Relationship to my master's research}

At Simon Fraser University, my master's research has asked how interfaces
make a person's state perceptible to others. In a cooperative Unity game, I
rendered remote teammates' heart rate and heart-rate variability as
label-free ambient icons; in a second project, I built a Unity environment
that responds to the player's heart rate. Both manuscripts are under revision
following peer review
\citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms,jahandideh_bioterrarium_ms}.

The teammate study left me with a question I could not answer inside it.
Participants tied their willingness to use the cue to control over it: they
asked for opt-in sharing, an immediate off switch and separate permissions
for the icon and the numbers \citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms}. Looking back as
the designer, I also noticed that the classification behind the label-free
display still sorted people into evaluative categories they never saw. Both
observations lead to the question this PhD takes up: who controls how a
shared system interprets people?

The PhD does not continue physiological sensing. It moves from involuntary
bodily signals to deliberate creative contributions, and from consent to
being made visible to the negotiation of being transformed. My training fits
this shift. In architecture and landscape architecture I learned to design
how people perceive and move through space; in HCI I have built and evaluated
networked interactive systems myself. That combination prepares me to design
a shared environment as a creative work, and then to study how people
interpret it.

\section{Program and supervisor fit}

The project belongs to the program's Technology in Practice area, with Media
\& Culture as a minor. It approaches AI-mediated creative work as a cultural
practice of participation, recognition and authorship, rather than as a
technical problem.

Dr.\ Caines's research on improvisation as a research methodology
\citep{caines2020fragile} and on socially responsive creative technologies
\citep{caines2023consultation} speaks directly to the project's setting and
methods. Her recent research-creation study of generative AI in
interdisciplinary classrooms attends to ethics, access, collaboration and
improvisatory skills \citep{caines2026steaming}. My project carries that
concern from learning with these tools to contribution-level recognition and
contested authorship in repeated group improvisation.
