\section{Problem and cultural stakes}

In collaborative creative tools, a generative model does more than produce
content: it \emph{reinterprets} what people contribute. Someone types a
phrase, and a moment later it has become a sound or a moving form in a shared
scene. The group then has to decide what that transformation means, and
whether it still belongs to the person who offered it.

This is a communication problem. \citet{hancock2020} define AI-mediated
communication as ``interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent
operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating
messages to accomplish communication goals,'' with examples such as smart
replies and auto-completion. In a shared creative work, the ``message'' is an
artistic offer, its audience is the rest of the group, and the AI's
modification becomes part of a collective artwork. When AI translates
contributions in this way, its outputs may become more visible or influential
than the contributions that prompted them, a possibility to investigate
rather than an established effect. This raises a cultural question about
whose interpretations gain authority, whose contributions remain
recognisable, and how disagreement is negotiated. Creative platforms
increasingly record who supplied what; this project asks whether such records
settle questions of credit.

What is new is the object of study. The closest studies
(Table~\ref{tab:related}) mostly position AI as a partner, performer or group
resource that people create \emph{with}. This project studies AI as a \emph{medium}
that people create \emph{through}, a translator between collaborators whose
reinterpretations they must accept, revise or refuse, and asks what that does
to recognition and authorship over repeated collective work.

Improvisation is a good place to ask. Improvisers already have conventions for
listening to, building on and refusing one another's offers, and improvisation
has been argued to be a research method grounded in risk, active listening and
collaborative response \citep{caines2020fragile}. A system that transforms
and labels offers enters those conventions, and may quietly change them.

Notably, \citet{xu2026} coded ``unacknowledged contributions'' as one kind
of breakdown. Contestability, developed mainly for high-stakes decisions such
as hiring or sentencing \citep{lyons2021}, offers a related vocabulary for
challenging a system.

Communication scholarship offers two vocabularies. Human--machine
communication asks how people make sense of AI as a communicator in its own
right \citep{guzman2020}; AI-mediated communication asks what happens when AI
alters messages between people \citep{hancock2020}. This project draws on
both, since participants may treat the system as a partner in one moment and
as a channel distorting a colleague's offer in the next. Cultural scholarship
explains why recognition matters.
\citet[p.~3]{jenkins2006} define a participatory culture as one in which
``members believe their contributions matter, and feel some degree of social
connection with one another''; this study asks what happens to that belief
when a model, rather than a peer, first responds to a contribution.
\citet{chalk2022} treats Actual Play, improvised tabletop play performed as
media, as cultural production in which few producers earn a living and
non-economic values such as participation, enjoyment and community help
motivate the work. Recognition is thus part of what participants receive for
their labour. \citet{neville2026} shows how technologies are rolled out into
settings whose practices outsiders misrecognise as non-technical;
improvisers' conventions for crediting and reusing offers are such practices,
and my system's attribution rules could override them. I take from these
works the stakes of recognition, and question whether a system's record of
contribution can stand in for it.

\section{Key terms and research questions}

Three working definitions organise the study. \textbf{Recognition}: a
contribution is noticed or acknowledged as meaningful. \textbf{Creative
authority}: a person's interpretation or decision carries influence over what
happens next. \textbf{Authorship}: participants associate ownership, credit
or responsibility with the work, as felt ownership, public attribution and
creative responsibility. Legal ownership and copyright are outside the scope
of this study. These can come apart: a contributor may be credited while the
group reshapes the material regardless of their view (credit without
influence), and a facilitator, interpreter or I as designer may shape the
work uncredited (influence without credit). The definitions are sensitising
concepts, not fixed categories, and listening, facilitation, silence and
refusal count as participation.

\textbf{Central question.} In collaborative digital improvisation, how do
participants, facilitators and a generative-AI interface shape whether a
person's contribution is recognised, carries creative authority, and is
credited in the shared work?

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Recognition.} How do participants notice, interpret and respond
        to differences between what they offered and how it was transformed?
  \item \emph{Authority.} Through which practices, including acceptance,
        revision, refusal, facilitation and silence, do contributors and
        groups gain or lose influence over transformed material, and how do
        interface permissions shape those practices?
  \item \emph{Authorship.} When and why do the system's technical attribution
        and participants' accounts of ownership, credit and responsibility
        diverge?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical framework}

My primary framework is Lucy Suchman's study of human--machine
reconfiguration \citep{suchman2007}, which examines how agencies are figured
at the interface and how humans and machines come to be treated as similar or
different in practice. On this view, whether a transformed element belongs to
its contributor, to the group or to the system is worked out in interaction.

In the illustrative episode in Section~\ref{sec:method}, the system records
that an amber pulse came from one participant's phrase, but the pulse also
depends on the model's reading, my asset library and the approval rule. When
the contributor refuses a request to brighten it, they enact an authority the
interface granted, which the group can accept or contest. The question shifts
from ``who really made the pulse?'' to how agency is distributed and
attributed in that moment: how participants attribute agency in talk and
action (``the AI did that'', ``that one is mine''), how rules, model defaults,
facilitation and my design decisions make some actions easy and others
difficult, and who is held responsible when a transformation misrepresents
someone. Improvisation studies adds a limited lens on how offers are taken up, ignored
or refused \citep{caines2020fragile}. These concepts tell me where to look,
not what I will find.

\section{Research-creation methodology}\label{sec:method}

The project follows the program's research-creation option, a creative work
with a 150--200-page written dissertation \citep{comcult_rc}. It is closest
to what \citet{chapman2012} call ``research-from-creation'': making the work
generates material for understanding how participants engage with it.

\textbf{The creative work.} The creative component will comprise the shared
audiovisual environment and the improvised works produced through it.
Developing and revising its expressive possibilities, interaction rules and
workshop scores will form part of the inquiry into how creative authority is
distributed. Participants build and perform short projected improvisations of
light, form and sound, then discuss them; each rule the environment embodies
is a proposition about collective authorship that they can test, resist or
rewrite. In an illustrative session, four
improvisers receive a score: build the weather of a city none of them has
visited. One types ``the bus that never comes.'' The system proposes a slow
amber pulse at the edge of the space with a low drone, with the generated
interpretation ``waiting as a distant repeating light.'' The contributor
accepts it. Later another participant wants the pulse brighter; the
contributor declines, and the facilitator asks the group how to proceed.
The scores are a creative output in their own right: a revised set of
improvisation scores for AI-mediated group work, credited to the groups that
shaped them with their consent, which other practitioners can use, adapt or
refuse. Because
the creative work must be available for the committee to assess
\citep{comcult_rc}, it will be documented through the executable environment,
these scores, selected consented recordings, and an annotated account of
design revisions. These materials will allow the committee to examine how the
creative process contributes to the dissertation's argument.

\textbf{Ownership and reuse.} Recording origins and giving contributors
approval rights could introduce individual ownership where improvisers treat
accepted offers as shared. Scoping interviews will investigate how
practitioners already understand ownership and reuse of improvised offers,
including whether accepted material becomes the group's and who decides that.
Co-design will examine whether contributor approval and visible attribution
support their practices or introduce individualised ownership assumptions
that participants do not share. One option to test is a way to ``release''
accepted material to the group.

\textbf{Phases and provisional scale.} Scoping interviews with improvisers and
facilitators come first. Co-design sessions with a few practitioners then set
the input form, the limits of transformation, the starting approval and
attribution rules, and accessibility provisions; these practitioners will not
join the workshop groups. A provisional design would then involve three
groups of three to five adult practitioners, each attending four workshops of
approximately 90--120 minutes, with recruitment seeking variation in
improvisational and creative-technology experience. I would aim to include
one existing ensemble and record prior relationships. Each participant then
has a reflection interview reviewing video of their own sessions. The final
design would be refined through scoping, access negotiations and ethics
review.

\textbf{Arrangements and what they support.} The first workshop uses only the
manual path, and the group writes down the procedure it will use when the
group decides. Each later workshop uses one arrangement from
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}, in a different order for each group, so each group
meets each arrangement once. What repeats is the group's evolving
collaboration across sessions, not trials within an arrangement. The
arrangements are situated contrasts whose effects cannot be separated from
order, prior relationships and emerging norms; I treat these as contextual
evidence. The human-mediated arrangement keeps contributor approval but also
changes timing and social relationships, so the study will not isolate a
causal effect of AI.

\textbf{Negotiating the rules.} The co-designed starting rules define each
arrangement. During a session, participants may negotiate how they apply
them, for instance by releasing material or overruling an approval;
substantial system changes are made only between sessions. Each rule version,
its reason and its consequences are recorded in the design journal. A changed
arrangement is not treated as equivalent to its earlier version, and the
change itself is analysed as evidence of negotiated authority.

\section{The system and the place of AI}

A participant submits a short text phrase. An existing language model
returns one \emph{proposal} drawn from a curated library of forms, light,
colour, particle ``weather'', sound and text, with a one-line \emph{generated
interpretation} that is model output, not an explanation of why the model
produced the transformation. Contributors or groups can accept, edit, reject,
request another proposal or undo, and a manual path to the same library is
always open. Generative AI is included because
machine-mediated reinterpretation is the object of study; the project does
not presume that AI improves improvisation. The model does not decide whether anything enters the work, who is credited
or what is removed, and makes no inferences about participants' emotions or
behaviour.

Neither the model nor the library is a neutral container. Documented
challenges include stereotyped images from text-to-image systems
\citep{bianchi2023}, value profiles in five GPT models resembling
English-speaking and Protestant European survey responses \citep{tao2024},
and a preference for Western-associated entities among multilingual and
Arabic models prompted in Arabic \citep{naous2024}. A 2022 benchmark also
found models of that time below human accuracy on paired figurative phrases
\citep{liu2022}. These results concern specific models and tasks, and I will
not benchmark models. I will record how metaphor, idiom and culturally
specific phrases are read when participants use them, discuss misreadings
with them, and examine how my asset choices privilege particular
associations.

\textbf{Feasibility.} Unity applies only permitted, bounded actions; model
output is never run as code. I will first test a local open-weight model,
otherwise an approved hosted service receiving only the phrase. No model is
trained.

\section{Case-study context and access}

Subject to discussion with Dr.\ Caines, these workshops could examine how
improvisation helps participants question and reshape the authority built
into creative technologies, a question close to the title of her SSHRC
Insight project, \emph{Interfacing, Playtesting, Resetting:
Reframing Technology Use Through Improvised Creative Technologies}
\citep{yfile2026sshrc}. Any relationship to that project would be determined
together; I do not assume it includes this study or any AI component. Otherwise I would use open calls to Toronto improvisation,
theatre and sound communities and to AMPD students, excluding anyone I teach.
As in \citeauthor{chalk2022}'s \citeyearpar{chalk2022} study, recruitment
would be iterative, checking whose practices are missing after the first
group and reporting remaining gaps. I will
not work with disability-focused or Indigenous communities without
established partnerships, community governance and relevant expertise.

\section{Analysis}

I will construct multimodal accounts of selected episodes, linking video of
participants' words and actions with screen recordings and the system's log.
An episode runs from a contribution, through its transformation and the
group's response, to the next creative action. Episode selection will include
routine acceptance and non-response as well as explicit disagreement. I will
document selection decisions and compare episodes across groups, arrangements
and stages of collaboration, including cases that challenge emerging
interpretations. The episode analysis adapts the video microethnography
\citet{mcarthur2015} used to study interface interactions; reflection
interviews are analysed through reflexive thematic analysis \citep{braun2019},
whose themes contextualise an episode without settling what it meant. System
records show what happened in the software, not intention. I will keep an analytic trail rather than seek inter-rater agreement,
and the design journal records how my choices as designer and facilitator
shape what participants can do.

\section{Ethics, consent and accessibility}

Consent will be specific, ongoing and revisable
\citep{im2021,strengers2021}, given separately for recording, AI processing,
use of recordings in the examination, and public dissemination; public
sharing is optional. Provenance will support identifying and, where feasible,
removing attributable contributions. Participants will agree on withdrawal
procedures and deadlines before the workshops, including the limits of
removing contributions from jointly developed or publicly disseminated works.
Credit is agreed with participants, whose time is paid; data are minimised,
pseudonymised and kept for stated periods. Full participation without AI is a
design commitment the workshops will test, through reduced motion, resizable
text, alternatives to sound-only information, facilitator-operated controls
and the manual path. Participation alone does not make a project equitable
\citep{birhane2022}.

\section{Intended contribution}

The project investigates whether technical attribution and social recognition
can diverge in collective creative work. A provenance log may record who
supplied a phrase while collaborators treat the transformed material as
another person's, the group's, or nobody's. The study will examine how
contributors gain or lose authority over transformed material, and how
interface permissions, facilitation and group conventions shape that
process. Its intended contribution is an explanation of these relationships,
rather than a general claim that more individual control produces fairer
collaboration. The evidence may help explain credit without influence,
influence without credit, how accepted material becomes collective, and when
provenance fails to resolve authorship disputes; these are possibilities, not
predicted findings. For communication studies, the project would extend
AI-mediated communication from messages between individuals to collective
creative production, where the mediated ``message'' becomes shared work. For
improvisation practice, it would offer scores, tested in the workshops, for
working with, around and against AI mediation. The resulting concepts may help interpret other platforms
that rely on attribution records, without establishing how those platforms
behave. If AI proves unnecessary in some arrangements, the contribution would
lie in explaining when and why.

\section{Relationship to my master's research}

My master's research at Simon Fraser University asks how interfaces make a
person's state perceptible to others, through label-free heart-rate cues for
remote teammates in a cooperative Unity game and a Unity environment that
responds to the player's heart rate. The teammate study is accepted at ACM
ISS 2026 \citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms},
and the second manuscript is under revision following peer review
\citep{jahandideh_bioterrarium_ms}. In the teammate
study, participants tied their willingness to use the cue to control over it,
asking for opt-in sharing, an immediate off switch and separate permissions
\citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms}. As the designer, I also noticed that the
classification behind the display still sorted people into evaluative
categories they never saw. Both lead to this PhD's question, who controls how
a shared system interprets people, now asked of deliberate creative
contributions rather than bodily signals.

\section{Program and supervisor fit}

The project belongs to Technology in Practice, with Media \& Culture as a
minor. Dr.\ Caines's work on improvisation as a research methodology
\citep{caines2020fragile} and socially responsive creative technologies
\citep{caines2023consultation} speaks to its setting and methods. In her
research-creation study of generative AI in classrooms, she came to see her
work with AI tools ``as a kind of critical improvisation practice,'' one
involving ``a deep responsibility to how this material is utilized'' and ``a
constant cycle of navigating power, adaptation, surprise, failure, and
creation'' \citep[p.~193]{caines2026steaming}. That study centred on course
design; this project asks how that responsibility and power are negotiated
among improvisers when the material is one another's contributions.
