\section{Problem and cultural stakes}

In collaborative creative tools, a generative model does more than produce
content: it \emph{reinterprets} what people contribute. Someone types a
phrase, and a moment later it has become a sound or a moving form in a shared
scene. The group then has to decide what that transformation means, and
whether it still belongs to the person who offered it.

When AI translates creative contributions into shared audiovisual material,
its outputs may become more visible or influential than the contributions
that prompted them, a possibility to investigate rather than an established
effect. This raises a cultural question about whose interpretations gain
authority, whose contributions remain recognisable, and how disagreement is
negotiated in collective creative practice. Creative platforms increasingly
record who supplied what; this project asks whether such records settle
questions of credit.

Improvisation is a good place to ask. Improvisers already have conventions for
listening to, building on and refusing one another's offers, and improvisation
has been argued to be a research method grounded in risk, active listening and
collaborative response \citep{caines2020fragile}. A system that transforms
and labels offers enters those conventions, and may quietly change them.

Several elements already exist: group negotiation around a shared AI, with
``unacknowledged contributions'' coded as a breakdown \citep{xu2026}; AI
reshaping social roles in co-creation \citep{suh2021}; and improvisation
informing AI partners and performers \citep{trajkova2024,mathewson2017}. The
extension lies in the combination:
following identifiable contributions through AI-mediated transformation in
\emph{repeated} collective improvisation, and asking whether technical
attribution and socially negotiated recognition come apart. Contestability,
mainly developed for high-stakes decisions such as hiring or sentencing
\citep{lyons2021}, offers a related vocabulary. Before the formal proposal I
will extend this base into communication and cultural scholarship on
collective authorship and creative labour.

\section{Key terms and research questions}

Three working definitions organise the study. \textbf{Recognition}: a
contribution is noticed or acknowledged as meaningful. \textbf{Creative
authority}: a person's interpretation or decision carries influence over what
happens next. \textbf{Authorship}: participants associate ownership, credit
or responsibility with the work, as felt ownership, public attribution and
creative responsibility; legal ownership and copyright are outside the scope
of this study.
These can come apart: a contributor may be credited on screen while the group
reshapes the material regardless of their view (credit without influence),
and a facilitator, an interpreter or I as designer may shape the work
uncredited (influence without credit). The definitions are sensitising
concepts, not fixed categories. I focus on the
trajectory of identifiable creative contributions while also attending to
listening, facilitation, silence and refusal as forms of participation.
Recognition is examined through participants' interpretations and
interactions rather than inferred solely from incorporation into the
artifact. I do not assume that group decision-making is fair, that
individual control is enough, or that facilitation does harm.

\textbf{Central question.} In collaborative digital improvisation, how do
participants, facilitators and a generative-AI interface shape whether a
person's contribution is recognised, carries creative authority, and is
credited in the shared work?

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Recognition.} How do participants notice, interpret and respond
        to differences between what they offered and how it was transformed?
  \item \emph{Authority.} Through which practices, including acceptance,
        revision, refusal, facilitation and silence, do contributors and
        groups gain or lose influence over transformed material, and how do
        interface permissions shape those practices?
  \item \emph{Authorship.} When and why do the system's technical attribution
        and participants' accounts of ownership, credit and responsibility
        diverge?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical framework}

My primary framework is Lucy Suchman's study of human--machine
reconfiguration \citep{suchman2007}, which examines how agencies are figured
at the interface and how humans and machines come to be treated as similar or
different in practice. On this view, whether a transformed element belongs to
its contributor, to the group or to the system is worked out in interaction.

Take the illustrative episode in Section~\ref{sec:method}. The system records
that an amber pulse came from one participant's phrase, but the pulse also
depends on the model's reading, my asset library and the approval rule. When
the contributor refuses a request to brighten it, they enact an authority the
interface granted, which the group can accept or contest. The question shifts
from ``who really made the pulse?'' to how agency is distributed and
attributed in that moment. Three questions follow: how participants attribute agency and
authorship in talk and action (``the AI did that'', ``that one is mine''); how
interface rules, model defaults, facilitation and my design decisions make
some actions easy and others difficult; and who is held responsible when a
transformation misrepresents someone. Improvisation studies adds a limited
lens on listening and on how offers are taken up, ignored or refused
\citep{caines2020fragile}. These concepts tell me where to look, not what I
will find.

\section{Research-creation methodology}\label{sec:method}

The project follows the program's research-creation option, a creative work
with a 150--200-page written dissertation \citep{comcult_rc}. It is closest
to what \citet{chapman2012} call ``research-from-creation'': making the work
generates material for understanding how participants engage with it.

\textbf{The creative work.} The creative component will comprise the shared
audiovisual environment and the improvised works produced through it.
Developing and revising its expressive possibilities, interaction rules and
workshop scores will form part of the inquiry into how creative authority is
distributed. Participants build and perform short collective improvisations,
projected scenes of light, form and sound, and then discuss them. Each rule
the environment embodies (who may approve, what is labelled, which assets
exist) is a proposition about collective authorship that participants can
test, resist or rewrite; this is how the work produces knowledge, not only
data.

In an illustrative session, four improvisers receive a score: build the
weather of a city none of them has visited. One types ``the bus that never
comes.'' The system proposes a slow amber pulse at the edge of the space with
a low drone, with the generated interpretation ``waiting as a distant
repeating light.'' The contributor accepts it. Later another participant
wants the pulse brighter; the contributor declines, and the facilitator asks
the group how to proceed. Such moments of acceptance, challenge and
resolution are what the study examines.

Because the creative work must be available for the committee to assess
\citep{comcult_rc}, it will be documented through the executable environment, workshop
scores, selected consented recordings, and an annotated account of design
revisions. These materials will allow the committee to examine how the
creative process contributes to the dissertation's argument. A public
sharing is possible but not essential.

\textbf{Does the system manufacture ownership?} Recording where contributions
came from, and giving contributors approval rights, could encourage
individual ownership where improvisers usually treat offers as shared. Do
practitioners credit individual offers? Does acceptance make material
available to the group, or must others ask again? Who decides when an
element has become collective? Scoping interviews will investigate how
practitioners already understand ownership and reuse of improvised offers.
Co-design will examine whether contributor approval and visible attribution
support their practices or introduce individualised ownership assumptions
that participants do not share. The rules in Figure~\ref{fig:overview} are
therefore provisional; one option to test is a way to ``release'' accepted
material to the group.

\textbf{Phases and provisional scale.} Scoping interviews with improvisers and
facilitators come first. Co-design sessions with a few practitioners then
set the input form, the limits of transformation, the approval and
attribution rules and accessibility provisions; these practitioners will not
join the workshop groups. A provisional design would then involve
three groups of three to five adult practitioners, each attending four
workshops of approximately 90--120 minutes, with recruitment seeking
variation in improvisational and creative-technology experience. Three groups
allow comparison across compositions; four sessions give norms time to form.
I would aim to include one existing ensemble and record prior relationships,
since familiarity likely affects how freely people refuse one another. Each
participant then has a reflection interview reviewing video of their own
sessions. The final design would be refined through scoping, access
negotiations and ethics review.

\textbf{Arrangements.} In the first workshop the group explores the
environment through the manual path and writes down the procedure it will use
when the group decides. Each later workshop uses one arrangement from
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}, in a different order for each group. The
contributor-led and group-led arrangements explore different distributions
of approval authority. The human-mediated arrangement keeps contributor
approval and offers an additional interpretive contrast, but it also changes
timing, social relationships and expectations, so the workshops support
situated comparison rather than isolate a causal effect of AI. Varying the
order does not remove carry-over; a group that learns to refuse in one
arrangement may refuse more readily in the next, and I treat such
developments as evidence of how conventions of authority form. Procedure
changes and facilitator interventions are recorded in session notes, the
system record and video.

\section{The system and the place of AI}

A participant submits a short text phrase (sketches may follow if co-design
supports them). An existing language model returns one \emph{proposal}, a
choice of elements and settings from a curated library of forms, light,
colour, particle ``weather'', sound and text, with a one-line
\emph{generated interpretation} everyone can see. That line is model output,
not an explanation of why the model produced the transformation. Depending on
the arrangement, contributors or groups can inspect, edit, accept, reject,
request another proposal or undo, and a manual path to the same library is
always open. Generative AI is included because machine-mediated
reinterpretation is the object of study. The project does not presume that AI
improves improvisation; it examines how participants respond to its
transformations and negotiate their place in the shared work. The model does
not decide whether anything enters the work, who is credited or what is
removed, and makes no inferences about participants' emotions or behaviour.

Neither the model nor the library is a neutral container. Research on
generative models raises concerns about stereotyped representation
\citep{bianchi2023}. Widely used language models have been shown to reflect
cultural values resembling English-speaking and Protestant European countries
\citep{tao2024} and to favour Western-associated entities in multilingual
use \citep{naous2024}, and they still trail people in interpreting
figurative phrases \citep{liu2022}. I will therefore examine how both model
interpretations and my asset choices privilege particular associations,
record how metaphor, idiom and non-English phrases are read, and discuss
misreadings with their authors. Conclusions will concern the people in the
study, not cultures or communities it does not include.

\textbf{Feasibility.} The technical design is deliberately modest, so that
effort goes into the expressive and social rules under study. Unity applies
only permitted actions with bounded settings; model output is checked against
them and never run as code. I will first test a local open-weight model to
avoid third-party processing, otherwise an institutionally approved hosted
service receiving only the phrase, without training or fine-tuning any model.

\section{Case-study context and access}

Subject to discussion with Dr.\ Caines, these workshops could examine how
improvisational practices help participants question and reshape the
authority built into creative technologies, a question close to the title of
her SSHRC Insight project, \emph{Interfacing, Playtesting, Resetting:
Reframing Technology Use Through Improvised Creative Technologies}
\citep{yfile2026sshrc}. The particular setting and relationship to her funded
project would be determined collaboratively. Only its title and funding are
public, and I do not assume it includes this study, these participants or any
AI component.

Otherwise I would use open calls to Toronto improvisation, theatre and sound
communities and to AMPD students, excluding anyone I teach. As in
\citeauthor{chalk2022}'s \citeyearpar{chalk2022} study of Actual Play
producers, recruitment would be iterative: after the first group I would
check whose practices are missing, adjust later calls, and report remaining
gaps. I will not work with disability-focused or Indigenous communities
without established partnerships, community governance and relevant
expertise.

\section{Analysis}

I will construct multimodal accounts of selected episodes, linking video of
participants' words and actions with screen recordings and the system's log.
An episode runs from a contribution, through its transformation and the
group's response, to the next creative action.
Episode selection will include routine acceptance and non-response as well as
explicit disagreement. I will document selection decisions and compare
episodes across groups, arrangements and stages of collaboration, including
cases that challenge emerging interpretations.

The episode analysis adapts the video microethnography \citet{mcarthur2015}
used to study players' interactions with interfaces. Reflection interviews
are analysed through reflexive thematic analysis \citep{braun2019}; their
themes show how participants describe ownership and authority in their own
terms, contextualising an episode without settling what it meant. System
records show what happened in the software, not a ``true'' account of
intention against which participants are judged. Discrepant cases, such as a group that ignores the attribution
display entirely, either revise an emerging explanation or limit its scope.
In keeping with a reflexive approach, I will keep an analytic trail rather
than seek inter-rater agreement, and the design journal records how my
choices as designer and facilitator shape what participants can do.

\section{Ethics, consent and accessibility}

Consent will be specific, ongoing and revisable
\citep{im2021,strengers2021}, given separately for recording, AI processing,
use of recordings in the examination, and public dissemination. Provenance
will support identifying and, where feasible, removing attributable
contributions. Participants will agree on withdrawal procedures and
deadlines before the workshops, including the limits of removing
contributions from jointly developed or publicly disseminated works. Credit
is agreed with participants, whose time is paid; data are minimised,
pseudonymised and kept for stated periods. Full participation without AI is a
design commitment the workshops will test, through reduced motion, resizable
text, alternatives to sound-only information, facilitator-operated controls
and the manual path. Participation alone does not make a project equitable
\citep{birhane2022}.

\section{Intended contribution}

The project investigates whether technical attribution and social
recognition can diverge in collective creative work. A provenance log may
record who supplied a phrase while collaborators treat the transformed
material as belonging to another person, to the group, or to nobody in
particular. The study will examine how contributors gain or lose authority
over transformed material, and how interface permissions, facilitation and
group conventions shape that process. Its intended contribution is an
explanation of these relationships, rather than a general claim that more
individual control produces fairer collaboration.

This is a proposition to investigate, not a finding. RQ3 asks whether and
when divergence occurs; RQ1 and RQ2 trace the recognition and authority that
would produce it. If divergence appears, the account would reach beyond these
workshops, to creative platforms that rely on attribution records and the
improvised conventions they meet; if not, the study would explain why the
two stayed aligned. If AI proves unnecessary in some arrangements, the
contribution would lie in explaining when, how and why, and what that
reveals about collective creativity.

\section{Relationship to my master's research}

My master's research at Simon Fraser University asks how interfaces make a
person's state perceptible to others, through label-free heart-rate cues for
remote teammates in a cooperative Unity game and a Unity environment that
responds to the player's heart rate. Both manuscripts are under revision
following peer review
\citep{jahandideh_bioterrarium_ms,jahandideh_teammate_ms}. In the teammate
study, participants tied their willingness to use the cue to control over it,
asking for opt-in sharing, an immediate off switch and separate permissions
\citep{jahandideh_teammate_ms}. As the designer, I also noticed that the
classification behind the display still sorted people into evaluative
categories they never saw. Both lead to this PhD's question, who controls how
a shared system interprets people, now asked of deliberate creative
contributions rather than bodily signals. My path from architecture and
landscape architecture to building and evaluating networked systems in HCI
prepares me to design a shared environment as a creative work and to study
how people interpret it.
